# The six ingredients — observable checks

Adapted from Conversion Rate Experts, *Making Websites Win* ("the ingredients of a
winning website"), turned into checks you can run against a page and get the same
answer twice. Each check is written so the answer is **met / not met / not
applicable**, not a judgement call.

## How to score

1. Run every check for the ingredient.
2. Mark each **met**, **not met**, or **n/a** (genuinely does not apply to this
   page type — not "hard to tell"; if you cannot tell from the input, mark it
   *unassessed* and say so).
3. Score = checks met ÷ checks applicable.

| Proportion met | Band |
|---|---|
| ≥ 85% | Strong |
| 60–84% | Adequate |
| 30–59% | Weak |
| < 30% | Absent |

Report the band **and** the raw count (e.g. "Adequate — 6/10"). The failed checks
are your findings; do not invent findings outside them.

Ten checks per ingredient, sixty in total. A page scoring above 80% overall is
performing near the ceiling of what page structure alone can do — further gains
come from the offer and the traffic, not the layout.

## Contents

1. Makes the benefits clear
2. Has an irresistible offer
3. Is trustworthy
4. Is written well
5. Is user-friendly
6. Gives people what they want

## 1. Makes the benefits clear

**What it means.** Within the first screen a visitor should know what is on offer,
what it does for *them*, and why it beats the alternative they are currently using
— expressed as outcomes, not features or brand language.

### Checks

1. **Category is named.** A visitor can say what kind of thing this is from the
   first screen, in plain words, without prior brand knowledge.
2. **The headline names an outcome or a change**, not the brand, the technology or
   an abstraction.
3. **A point of difference against the visitor's current alternative is stated**
   somewhere in the first two screens.
4. **A subhead translates the mechanism into a benefit** in one or two sentences,
   rather than listing specs alone.
5. **At least one lead benefit is quantified** with a number, a time, or a
   countable scope.
6. **A scannable benefit or spec strip appears within the first two screens** —
   three to six short claims a visitor can absorb without reading prose.
7. **The lead benefit continues the promise of the traffic source** (ad, email,
   search query). Scent is unbroken.
8. **Nothing in the first screen requires decoding** — no internal product names,
   sub-brand hierarchies or coined terms used before they are explained.
9. **The benefit is the visitor's, not the product's.** Claims are phrased around
   what changes for them, not what the product contains or does.
10. **The first screen makes a single argument.** It is not a carousel of
    competing propositions the visitor must choose between.

### Common failure modes

- Leading with the brand or the technology name before the visitor knows the
  benefit ("brand throat-clearing").
- Feature-as-benefit: a spec stated as if it self-evidently matters, with no
  translation into what the visitor gets.
- A clever headline that wins on tone but says nothing a sceptic can act on.
- One generic hero shown to every segment, so it lands for none.
- A hero carousel rotating three propositions, none of which gets read.

### Diagnostic questions

- Five-second test: after one screen, can a visitor say what this is, what it does
  for them, and why it's different?
- Is the primary benefit an outcome, or a feature dressed as one?
- Would this headline still make sense with a competitor's logo on it? If yes,
  check 3 has failed.

## 2. Has an irresistible offer

**What it means.** The offer is the total of what the visitor gets, what they pay,
the terms, the risk they carry, and any bonus — architected so yes is easy and no
feels like leaving value behind. The product can be excellent and the offer still
weak. Full mechanics in `offer-architecture.md`.

### Checks

1. **Price is reframed into the unit the visitor thinks in** where a unit exists —
   per serving, per use, per day, per wash. Not only the basket price.
2. **What's included is itemised**, component by component, not summarised as a
   category.
3. **Included components carry a stated individual worth**, and the arithmetic
   reconciles to the price paid, with the method explained.
4. **Any discount has a stated reason** — bundle, first order, seasonal, launch,
   subscription — rather than appearing arbitrary.
5. **Risk reversal is present and within one screen of the primary CTA** —
   guarantee, returns policy, warranty, trial, money-back.
6. **If there is a commitment, exit terms are stated at the decision point** in
   plain words ("cancel any time, no fees"), not only in the FAQ or footer.
7. **If there is a plan choice, one option is recommended and pre-selected**, with
   a stated reason for the recommendation.
8. **Recurring plans are framed as upside** — savings, flexibility, exclusives,
   early access — rather than as a concession the visitor tolerates.
9. **Bonuses and gifts carry a cash value**, so the visitor can price them.
10. **Thresholds are stated with the gap to close** ("spend £45 for free
    delivery"), not just the rule.

### Common failure modes

- The offer is just a price: no risk reversal, no bonus, no framing.
- Risk reversal exists but sits in the footer, so perceived risk stays high.
- Included value is real but never counted, so the visitor prices the headline
  item alone and finds it expensive.
- Subscription framed defensively ("you can cancel") rather than as the sensible
  default for a recurring need.
- Reaching for a discount or a countdown to manufacture urgency when surfacing
  existing value would do the same job without eroding margin or brand.

### Diagnostic questions

- At the point of decision, can the visitor see the full offer — price, terms,
  what's included, what happens if it doesn't work — without scrolling or clicking?
- What is the largest perceived risk here, and is it reversed on the page?
- Could this offer be made stronger *without* discounting? Usually yes: itemise
  the value, reframe the unit, reverse the risk harder.

## 3. Is trustworthy

**What it means.** The page earns belief fast enough to survive a sceptic. Trust is
the most commonly cited reason for not buying, so this ingredient usually carries
the most upside. Different doubts need different *kinds* of proof — the taxonomy
is in `proof-types.md`.

### Checks

1. **Volume proof appears with a real number** near the top — review count,
   customer count, units sold.
2. **At least one piece of third-party proof** the brand does not control — award,
   independent lab result, certification, press, retailer rating.
3. **Reviews are attributed and specific** — a name plus a location or verified
   status, and a concrete outcome rather than a generic compliment.
4. **At least one proof point addresses the page's hardest objection head-on**,
   rather than only praising the product.
5. **The mechanism is explained** — why it works, in named parts a sceptic can
   follow, not just asserted.
6. **Authority is a person or institution with relevant standing**, quoted on the
   specific benefit rather than lending a face.
7. **Numbers are specific rather than round-and-vague** — an exact review count
   beats "thousands"; a stated test protocol beats "clinically proven".
8. **Every superlative is bounded or evidenced.** No unfalsifiable claim stands
   alone as the only support for a benefit.
9. **Composition is transparent** — ingredients, materials, specs, dimensions or
   nutritional data available on the page.
10. **The seller is identifiable** — founders, story, or a clear company identity,
    not an anonymous storefront.

### Common failure modes

- Adjective-led credibility ("advanced", "breakthrough", "revolutionary") with no
  evidence behind it.
- Proof present but placed far from the doubt it answers.
- A wall of five-star reviews that all praise the same easy thing, none of which
  addresses the actual objection.
- Borrowed authority with no relevance — a celebrity unconnected to the category,
  or a badge nobody recognises.
- Trust asked for at the moment of action, with nothing earned earlier.

### Diagnostic questions

- For every claim on the page, is there proof a sceptic could check?
- Which of the proof types in `proof-types.md` are present, and which of the
  visitor's actual doubts is each one answering?
- Is the proof specific, or atmospheric?

## 4. Is written well

**What it means.** Copy a visitor can read at a glance and be moved by: concrete,
specific, scannable, ordered as an argument, with objections answered where they
arise rather than left to fester.

### Checks

1. **Headings alone carry the argument.** Reading only the headings gives the
   full case.
2. **Claims are concrete and picturable** — they name a thing, a number, or a
   situation the visitor recognises.
3. **The copy uses the visitor's words for the problem**, not internal or category
   jargon.
4. **Sections are labelled by what the visitor wants** ("What's included", "How to
   use", "What's inside"), not by internal naming.
5. **A numbered reasons-to-buy block exists** — an explicit, enumerated list of
   why this, rather than reasons scattered through prose.
6. **Objections are answered where they arise**, adjacent to the claim or the
   price that triggers them, not quarantined in a footer FAQ.
7. **The FAQ answers doubts, not only logistics**, and is phrased in the
   visitor's own question form.
8. **Effort and time-to-value are stated explicitly** — how long it takes, how
   many steps, how soon they will notice something.
9. **Depth is available but not blocking** — detail sits behind accordions, tabs
   or "more details" so scanners and researchers are both served.
10. **Register is consistent with the brand's rules** (see the brand constraints
    file) throughout, including microcopy and button labels.

### Common failure modes

- Abstraction and hedging; filler adjectives doing the work numbers should do.
- Walls of text with no scannable hierarchy; the argument out of order.
- Objections ignored, or answered a full screen away from where they bite.
- A FAQ full of shipping and contact questions, doing no persuasion at all.
- Sections named after internal projects or product lines.

### Diagnostic questions

- Can the page be understood by scanning headings and first lines alone?
- Take the top three objections for this audience: where on the page is each
  answered, and is it near where the doubt arises?
- How long does the visitor think this will take to use? Is that stated, or left
  for them to fear?

## 5. Is user-friendly

**What it means.** A visitor can understand the page, find what they need, and
complete the one action with minimum friction, doubt and delay, on the device they
actually use. Structural patterns in `page-architecture.md`.

### Checks

1. **There is exactly one primary action**, and every CTA for it uses the **same
   words**.
2. **The primary CTA is visible in the first screen on mobile.**
3. **The CTA repeats after each persuasive block**, so a visitor convinced at any
   point can act without scrolling to hunt.
4. **A persistent CTA exists** — sticky bar, sticky buy box, or a docked button —
   on pages longer than about three screens.
5. **Long pages carry in-page anchor navigation** to the main sections.
6. **Price, plan, variant and quantity choices all happen in one place**, without
   a page change or a modal round trip.
7. **Variant choice is signposted** — bestseller, most popular, recommended — so
   an undecided visitor is guided rather than left to a flat grid.
8. **Delivery, returns and cost information is reachable from the buy box**
   without leaving the page.
9. **The form asks the minimum** for the commitment being made, and says what
   happens next.
10. **The page loads fast and stays stable** — no layout shift, images sized, no
    render-blocking media, no interstitial covering the hero on arrival.

### Common failure modes

- More than one competing primary action, so the visitor must first choose what
  to do.
- CTA below the fold only, or styled as a secondary element.
- Full site navigation on a paid-traffic landing page, leaking visitors sideways.
- A popup covering the hero within seconds of arrival.
- Information not modular: the visitor cannot find the objection-handler and,
  once lost, never sees it.
- A form asking for data the business will not use, before the visitor has
  committed to anything.

### Diagnostic questions

- On mobile, is the one thing to do obvious within a second, and reachable
  without hunting?
- How many taps and fields to convert? Which can be removed or deferred?
- Where can a visitor accidentally leave this page, and is that exit worth it?

## 6. Gives people what they want

**What it means.** The page matches what *this* visitor came for — their intent,
their segment, their stage of readiness, and the next step they are willing to
take. A page can be clear, trustworthy and well-built and still lose because it
answers a question this visitor was not asking.

### Checks

1. **The page states who it is for** in the first screen, explicitly or by
   unmistakable implication.
2. **A decision-support tool exists for the "which one suits me" question** —
   quiz, finder, comparison table, "help me choose", shade or size matcher.
3. **Multiple use cases or occasions are named**, so different visitors can locate
   themselves in the page.
4. **Choice is bounded** — a recommended default or a curated shortlist exists
   rather than an undifferentiated grid.
5. **Depth is layered**: a thirty-second path and a ten-minute path both exist and
   both end at the CTA.
6. **A secondary conversion exists for the not-ready** — email capture with a
   stated incentive, quiz entry, referral, notify-me.
7. **Post-purchase anxieties are addressed on the page** — how to use it, how to
   care for it, when it arrives, how to change or cancel.
8. **The action asked for matches the visitor's readiness and the funnel stage** —
   no hard purchase ask to cold, pre-launch traffic.
9. **Objections specific to this audience are handled**, not a generic set.
10. **No qualified-but-different visitor hits a dead end** — someone who wants a
    related variant, a different size, or a gift has somewhere to go.

### Common failure modes

- One hero for every segment; the message is generic and fits none.
- Asking for the wrong commitment for the stage.
- Mismatch between the ad or email promise and the page — broken scent.
- Treating a discovery-mode browser as a high-intent searcher, or the reverse.
- A large range presented flat, with no guidance, so choice paralysis substitutes
  for a decision.

### Diagnostic questions

- Does the hero and lead benefit match the segment this traffic is tagged as?
- If a visitor is interested but unsure which version suits them, what does the
  page give them?
- What is the next step for a visitor who is interested but not ready today?

## 7. Awareness and readiness (enrichment lens, not scored)

Run this lens across all six ingredients. The same page serves visitors at very
different stages, and the winning version meets each where they are.

**Stages:**

- **Unaware / problem-unaware** — does not think they have the problem. Needs the
  category reframed before any product pitch lands.
- **Problem-aware** — knows the issue, does not know the solution class. Needs
  education on what the fix even looks like.
- **Solution-aware** — knows this category exists, sceptical it works. Needs
  category contrast and specific proof.
- **Product-aware** — knows this brand, weighing it up. Needs the offer, risk
  reversal and objection-handling.
- **Most-aware** — warm click from a CRM email or a retargeting ad. Needs the path
  to action with minimum friction, not the full pitch again.

**How to use it.**

- Identify which stage(s) the traffic to this page is in.
- Check the page opens at the right altitude: too much pitch for a most-aware
  visitor wastes their patience; too little context for an unaware visitor loses
  them.
- A page that must serve a span of stages should order the argument so the ready
  can act immediately (CTA up top, repeated) while the sceptical can scroll into
  proof and objection-handling.
- A hypothesis that changes the *opening altitude* — for example a stripped
  most-aware variant for CRM traffic — is usually higher-impact than any single
  module tweak.
