# Proof types

"Add social proof" is not a finding. Proof types are not interchangeable — each
answers a different doubt, and a page can be covered in five-star reviews while
leaving its hardest objection completely unanswered.

Use this file two ways: to **inventory** what proof a page has, and to **match**
the missing proof to the doubt it would resolve.

---

## The taxonomy

### 1. Volume proof

Review counts, customer counts, units sold, members.

- **Answers:** "am I the only one?" / "is this a real company?"
- **Strong form:** an exact number, placed near the top and repeated near the CTA.
  Precision reads as a count; roundness reads as a claim.
- **Weak form:** "thousands of happy customers", "loved by many".
- **Ceiling:** volume proof establishes legitimacy but never resolves a specific
  doubt. A page that relies on it alone will lose the sceptic.

### 2. Attributed customer reviews

Individual reviews with a name, a location or verified-buyer status, and a
concrete outcome.

- **Answers:** "will it work for someone like me?"
- **Strong form:** a headline that names the benefit in the customer's words, then
  a review describing a specific situation and result. Attribution to a first name
  plus a place or a verified badge.
- **Weak form:** anonymous, generic praise. "Great product, love it."
- **The high-leverage move:** select reviews that answer objections, not reviews
  that praise. One review from someone with the exact reservation the visitor
  holds outperforms ten enthusiastic ones.

### 3. Third-party validation

Awards, independent lab results, certifications, standards, retailer ratings.

- **Answers:** "are they just saying that?"
- **Strong form:** named body, named award or test, and a date or protocol. A lab
  results table showing what was tested and what was found is the strongest
  version of this, because it invites checking.
- **Weak form:** an unexplained badge, a self-issued certification, an award from
  an organisation the visitor has never heard of and which is not explained.
- **Note:** this is the only proof type the brand does not control, which is
  exactly why it carries the most weight against a sceptic.

### 4. Press and editorial

Publication quotes and logos.

- **Answers:** "is this a serious brand?"
- **Strong form:** a real quoted sentence about the product, attributed to the
  publication. Quotes that make an argument beat logos that make a wall.
- **Weak form:** a logo bar with no quotes, including logos from a single passing
  mention or a paid placement.

### 5. Expert and practitioner endorsement

Professionals with standing in the relevant field: clinicians, scientists,
advisory boards, chefs, trainers.

- **Answers:** "does someone who knows this field think it's any good?"
- **Strong form:** a named person, their relevant credential, and a quote about
  the specific mechanism or benefit. An advisory board with named members and
  their institutions.
- **Weak form:** "developed with experts" with no expert named.
- **Watch:** register. Some audiences reject a clinical or white-coat presentation
  and respond to expert-as-peer instead. Check the brand constraints.

### 6. Celebrity and founder authority

A recognisable person attached to the product, or the founders themselves.

- **Answers:** "who is behind this, and do they know what they're doing?"
- **Strong form:** relevance plus specificity. A celebrity whose expertise is the
  category, quoted on the exact thing the product does. A founder story that
  explains why this product exists and what problem they personally had.
- **Weak form:** a famous face with no connection to the category, or a founder
  story that is only origin-mythology with no bearing on the product.
- **Note:** founder proof is available to every brand at zero cost and is
  routinely left off the page.

### 7. Mechanism proof

An explanation of *why* it works, in named, followable parts.

- **Answers:** "why should this work when the last one didn't?"
- **Strong form:** the mechanism broken into named components with a diagram or a
  step-by-step, plus named proprietary technology, patents or patent-pending
  status where genuine.
- **Weak form:** "advanced formula", "proprietary blend", asserted and unexplained.
- **This is the most under-used proof type.** For any product competing against a
  category the visitor has already been disappointed by, mechanism proof is what
  distinguishes "another one of those" from "a different thing".

### 8. Composition transparency

Ingredients, materials, specifications, dimensions, nutrition, sourcing.

- **Answers:** "what am I actually buying?" / "is there anything in here I avoid?"
- **Strong form:** every component listed, each with a line on what it does and why
  it is there. Full specs and dimensions for physical products.
- **Weak form:** a list with no explanation, or a summary that hides the detail.
- **Doubles as fit:** transparency lets a visitor with a restriction, an allergy or
  a specific requirement self-qualify instead of bouncing.

### 9. Process and provenance proof

Investment in getting it right: years of development, iterations tested, options
rejected, where and how it is made.

- **Answers:** "is this a serious product or a white-label?"
- **Strong form:** numbers. Years of R&D, variants tested, trials run.
- **Weak form:** "carefully crafted", "meticulously developed".

### 10. Demonstration

Video, before-and-after, side-by-side comparison, the product visibly doing the
thing.

- **Answers:** "show me."
- **Strong form:** unedited or plausibly-unedited demonstration of the specific
  benefit claimed, in a realistic setting.
- **Weak form:** a lifestyle film with no demonstration in it.
- **Note:** claims about a *visible* outcome should almost always be demonstrated
  rather than described. Written proof of a visual claim under-performs.

## Matching proof to doubt

Work the objection inventory (`diagnostics.md`) first, then choose the proof type
that actually resolves each entry:

| The visitor's doubt | Proof type that resolves it |
|---|---|
| "It won't work for me specifically" | Attributed reviews from similar people; fit tools |
| "It won't work at all" | Mechanism proof; third-party testing; demonstration |
| "They're exaggerating" | Third-party validation; specific bounded numbers |
| "This is a fly-by-night brand" | Volume proof; founder identity; press |
| "It's not worth the price" | Composition transparency; process proof; value itemisation |
| "It'll be complicated or messy" | Demonstration; how-to steps; time-to-value |
| "There's something in it I avoid" | Composition transparency; certifications |
| "I'll be stuck with it" | Risk reversal (an offer mechanic, not proof) |
| "Everyone in this category over-promises" | Mechanism proof plus category contrast |

## Auditing proof that isn't working

A page can be dense with proof and still fail this ingredient. Check for:

- **Proof orphaned from its doubt.** The guarantee is in the footer; the lab
  results sit below the fold on a page whose main objection is safety. Proof
  resolves a doubt only if it is adjacent to where the doubt arises.
- **Monoculture.** Six of the ten types missing, one type repeated at length.
  Usually all reviews, no mechanism.
- **Proof of the easy thing.** Every review praises the scent; nobody mentions
  whether it cleans. The objection is untouched.
- **Unfalsifiable proof.** Claims constructed so they could never be wrong, which
  a sceptical reader discounts entirely.
- **Borrowed credibility with no relevance.** Badges, logos and faces that signal
  effort rather than evidence.
- **Uncheckable specificity.** A precise-looking statistic with no source,
  protocol or date. This is worse than a vague claim, because being caught costs
  the whole page.

## Constraint interactions

Some brands cannot use some proof types. Check the constraints file before
recommending:

- Testimonials may be policy-blocked pending legal or regulatory sign-off.
- Efficacy statistics may be limited to an approved set, with all others banned.
- Competitor comparison may be blocked, which rules out side-by-side proof and
  forces category contrast phrased without names.
- Regulated categories may forbid the strongest available claim entirely,
  regardless of the evidence behind it.

When a proof type is blocked, say which one and recommend the strongest available
substitute rather than dropping the finding.
